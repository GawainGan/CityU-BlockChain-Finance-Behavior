Research Progress Memo · Plan B (V4-New_Branch)

Plan B: Risk Behavior and Credit Signals in Public DeFi Lending Data

To: PhD SupervisorDate: 2026-07-07Main line: DeFi lending behavior and risk signals based on public on-chain data
One-sentence positioning: I am exploring a new direction that does not depend on private commercial data: how borrowers actively adjust positions when on-chain lending risk rises, and whether these behavioral processes contain information beyond conventional risk indicators.

1Why I Am Considering a Shift from V3 to This Public-Data Direction

The main difficulty in V3 is not the theoretical question but the reproducibility of the empirical material. The original design depends on UnusPay’s deterministic mapping between on-chain transactions and off-chain orders. This type of data is highly valuable for research, but it also makes the paper’s verifiability, sample size, and external scrutiny depend on a single commercial partner. If supervisors or reviewers cannot independently reproduce the key data, even significant model results may be read as findings from one special platform rather than evidence that can enter a broader academic debate.

The starting point of V4-New_Branch is therefore to find a research setting that is fully public, reproducible, and still connected to the question of whether on-chain behavior carries credit information. DeFi lending protocols provide such a setting. Users deposit crypto assets as collateral and borrow another asset; when collateral prices fall, debt exposure increases, or the safety buffer becomes thinner, users must decide whether to add collateral, repay debt, reduce risk exposure, or maintain a risky position. These are not survey responses or platform-internal scores. They are real financial decisions recorded on-chain, and the transaction records can be independently retrieved by third parties.

At this stage, I am not primarily interested in the engineering design of DeFi protocols. I am interested in a more general behavioral question: when a borrower’s position gradually becomes risky, does the borrower exhibit stable patterns of response? Are these patterns merely mechanical reflections of the current risk state, or do they contain additional signals that help predict future risk? This question continues the V3 concern with whether observable on-chain behavior can become credit information, but it no longer relies on private off-chain labels or on a single platform’s commercial dataset.

2Necessary Background: What Can Be Observed in DeFi Lending

DeFi lending can be understood first as a collateralized loan market governed by smart contracts. Users do not need bank approval or conventional KYC credit documentation; they interact directly with protocols through wallet addresses. Protocols usually require borrowers to deposit collateral before borrowing another asset up to a fraction of collateral value. As long as collateral remains sufficiently large relative to debt, users can maintain the position, adjust collateral, increase borrowing, or repay debt.

This market has two features that matter for research. First, risk status changes continuously because collateral prices, debt value, and user actions all affect position safety. Second, user responses are observable because adding collateral, repaying debt, increasing borrowing, withdrawing collateral, switching assets, and being liquidated by a third party all leave on-chain records. In other words, we can observe not only whether a user ultimately enters distress, but also what the user did as risk accumulated and when those actions occurred.

This background distinguishes DeFi lending from traditional credit data. Traditional credit research often observes application information, repayment outcomes, and some account behavior, but it is difficult to observe every position adjustment near a risk boundary. DeFi makes these process data public: each address’s borrowing, repayment, collateral adjustment, and liquidation events can be reconstructed as a time series. This does not mean on-chain data reveal a user’s complete balance sheet, since the user may also act on centralized exchanges or through other wallets. But it does provide a reproducible window into the relationship between publicly visible behavior and later risk.

Recent literature already shows that DeFi lending risk is worth studying from multiple angles. Recent multi-chain risk-modeling work emphasizes that lending risk is expanding from single protocols to cross-chain and cross-protocol environments (Sevim, 2026). Institutional DeFi risk frameworks also treat collateral, liquidity, governance, and technical risk as dimensions that must be jointly assessed (Oberholzer & Zamaraiev, 2026). Research on automated risk mechanisms in Aave and Compound shows that DeFi lending protocols are rule-based, but their risk-handling mechanisms differ across protocols (Iftikhar, Wei, & Cartlidge, 2025). These studies provide institutional and risk-modeling background, but they usually focus on protocol risk, model risk, or parameter design rather than placing borrowers’ active behavior during risk accumulation at the center of the analysis.

3The Question I Actually Want to Ask

I want to narrow the question to one sentence: in public on-chain lending markets, do borrowers’ active adjustments under rising position risk provide risk information beyond conventional on-chain indicators? By conventional indicators, I mean relatively direct variables such as current collateral sufficiency, debt size, asset volatility, account activity, and historical liquidation records. My interest is whether behavioral processes such as how users respond, whether they respond late, whether they increase leverage when risk rises, or whether they resume risky activity after distress have explanatory or predictive value in their own right.

The question has two layers. The first is behavioral: when a position approaches a danger zone, do users exhibit systematic active adjustment patterns rather than random operations? The second is credit-oriented: can these behavioral patterns help predict future liquidation, bad-debt exposure, or a higher-risk position state? The first layer asks whether there is a behavior structure worth explaining. The second asks whether that structure is merely interesting or also useful for risk assessment.

Prospect theory should not be the opening slogan of this direction; it is a theoretical tool that may later organize behavioral hypotheses. If the data show nonlinear responses near risky regions, stronger remedial action or continued risk-taking in loss states, and weaker reactions farther from risk boundaries, prospect theory can help explain why these patterns are not simply rational risk control. At the current memo stage, however, I prefer first to clarify the research object: I am observing DeFi borrowers’ active on-chain behavior as position risk changes, rather than assuming in advance that the behavior must fit a particular theory.

4Core Framework and Main Risks

The core framework should keep only three steps rather than frontloading all technical details. The first step is to reconstruct position-risk trajectories: for each borrowing address, I would organize borrowing, collateral, repayment, price movement, and liquidation events over time, forming process data on how risk status changes. The reason for doing this is that final liquidation alone cannot distinguish a user who experienced a sudden price shock from a user who stayed in a risky zone for a long time without adjustment.

The second step is to distinguish active behavior from passive events. Active behavior refers to actions initiated by the borrower, such as adding collateral, repaying debt, increasing borrowing, withdrawing collateral, or adjusting assets. Passive events refer to liquidations triggered by third-party liquidators or bots. This distinction is the foundation of the design because I want to study how borrowers make decisions under risk pressure, not mistakenly treat liquidations executed by others as borrower behavior. If this distinction cannot be made reliably, any behavioral interpretation would collapse into mechanical event counting.

The third step is to test whether the behavioral process provides incremental risk information. After controlling for current risk status, asset type, account size, historical activity, and market volatility, I would add behavioral-process variables and compare whether prediction of future liquidation or risk deterioration improves. The expected output does not need to be a full credit-scoring system at the beginning. A more cautious output is to judge whether these behavioral variables contain independent information and whether that information is strong enough to justify more formal risk modeling later.

Main risks and downgrade paths: Position-risk trajectories may not be reconstructed with sufficient precision; if the error is large, the study should downgrade to coarser risk-zone analysis rather than precise threshold or discontinuity analysis. Active behavior may be sparse; if liquidation bots make the final window too short, the study should shift earlier to preventive behavior several days before liquidation. Contract wallets, multisig wallets, or proxy contracts may obscure who made the decision; if they cannot be parsed, the main sample should be limited to ordinary wallets with reliably identifiable initiators. Behavioral variables may also simply recode current risk; if they do not add stable predictive value, the project can remain a behavioral description paper, but it should not claim to improve credit assessment.

5What I Have Done (Recent Progress)

Recently, I have mainly done three types of preparatory work. First, I re-examined the data-dependence problem in V3 and confirmed that the value of V4 should not be to replace one dataset with another while keeping the same question. Instead, it should use public on-chain lending data to formulate a more reproducible behavioral and risk question. This shift reduces dependence on a commercial partner and makes the eventual paper easier for supervisors, reviewers, and other researchers to inspect independently.

Second, I conducted an initial mapping of observable event types in DeFi lending data, including borrowing, repayment, collateral deposits, collateral withdrawals, asset adjustment, and liquidation. This mapping is not yet a complete data validation exercise, but it helped identify a crucial operational issue: actions initiated by borrowers must be separated from liquidations triggered by third parties. This issue is more important than model choice because it determines whether the object of study is user decision-making or protocol mechanics.

Third, I completed a preliminary review of the literature and the project’s positioning. The most recent work already covers DeFi risk frameworks, multi-chain risk modeling, Aave/Compound risk mechanisms, on-chain credit scoring, and adaptive interest-rate mechanisms. But the specific question of whether borrowers’ active behavior during risk accumulation carries incremental risk information has not yet formed a clear research line. This is only a provisional judgment and still needs a more systematic literature search; it should not be packaged prematurely as a confirmed gap.

7Why This Is Worth Doing (Novelty and Literature Support)

The potential novelty of this direction is not that DeFi is risky, nor that on-chain data can predict liquidation. Those points are already being studied by many researchers. The more promising contribution is to move risk prediction from static state variables to behavioral process variables. Two borrowers in similarly risky zones may respond very differently: one may add collateral early and reduce leverage, while another may continue borrowing or remain inactive for a long period. Conventional risk indicators may place them close together, but their behavioral processes may reveal different approaches to risk management.

Relative to the latest literature, this direction can be positioned in a narrow but meaningful gap. Sevim (2026) and Oberholzer and Zamaraiev (2026) show that DeFi risk assessment is moving toward multi-chain, institutional, and multidimensional frameworks. Iftikhar, Wei, and Cartlidge (2025) focus on automated risk-management mechanisms in Aave and Compound. Ghosh et al. (2024) directly study on-chain credit risk scoring. Bastankhah et al. (2024) and Chitra (2025) discuss protocol improvement from adaptive interest rates or dynamic pricing. These studies provide background on how risk is modeled and how protocols may be tuned, but my entry point is more micro-level: given a risk mechanism, how do borrowers act, and is that action itself part of the risk signal?

From a behavioral-finance perspective, prospect theory and studies of cryptocurrency investment bias provide a possible interpretive framework, but I should not treat that framework as a conclusion at the opening stage. A more cautious path is first to show whether DeFi borrowers’ behavior near risk accumulation has a stable structure, and only then assess whether that structure can be interpreted as loss aversion, reference-point response, or changing risk preference. This way, even if the data do not fully fit prospect theory, the paper can still remain a valid empirical study of on-chain lending-risk behavior rather than failing because the theory was attached too early.

If the direction works, the output should be stated modestly: public on-chain lending data record not only risk outcomes but also the process through which risk forms; borrowers’ active behavior during risk accumulation may provide a supplementary perspective for understanding DeFi lending risk. This output does not need to promise an immediate redesign of Aave parameters or a new credit-scoring system, but it can provide a foundation for later work on protocol risk management, user segmentation, and cross-protocol risk migration.

8Claims I Will Not Overstate at This Stage

I will not claim that DeFi borrowers have already been shown to follow prospect theory, because that requires rigorous behavioral tests and the exclusion of alternative explanations. I will not claim that behavioral variables necessarily improve credit scoring, because they may simply duplicate collateral ratio, price volatility, and account activity. I will also not make cross-protocol behavioral consistency a main contribution, because without off-chain identity anchors, cross-protocol similarity can only show similar behavior; it cannot safely establish that two addresses belong to the same person.

The more appropriate framing at this stage is that I am exploring a public-data, reproducible direction that is related to the V3 line but does not rely on private data. The minimum viable contribution is to describe and test the active behavioral structure of DeFi borrowers as risk accumulates. The stronger contribution is to determine whether that structure contains risk information beyond conventional on-chain indicators. The next step should be to verify the data pipeline and behavioral classification on a small sample before deciding whether to develop the direction into a formal paper design.

Relationship to Plan A: The two plans use different data sources and empirical strategies, but they share the core interest in whether observable on-chain behavior can become credit information. Plan B is not a simple substitute for Plan A; it is an independently viable direction built around a different empirical anchor.
